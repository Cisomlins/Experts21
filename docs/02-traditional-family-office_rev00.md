# How a Traditional Family Office Works

> **Purpose:** Explain how human family offices are defined, organized, governed, staffed, priced and regulated in Brazil and the USA, and which practices an AI Family Office should copy. **Primary users:** the Orchestrator/Chief of Staff agent, the CIO agent, the Risk agent, the Regulation/Tax agent, the Wealth Planning/Succession agent, and the Reporting agent.

## Summary

- A **family office** is a private organization that manages the wealth and affairs of a wealthy family. A **single-family office (SFO)** serves one family; a **multi-family office (MFO)** serves several families; a **virtual family office** coordinates outside specialists with a very small in-house team.
- There were an estimated 8,030 SFOs worldwide in 2024 (up from about 6,130 in 2019), managing about US$3.1 trillion; Deloitte projects more than 10,720 by 2030 and US$5.4 trillion in assets [1]. South America had about 190 SFOs in 2024 [1].
- Core functions go well beyond investing: accounting and reporting, tax, estate and succession planning, legal, risk and insurance, philanthropy, lifestyle services, and family governance and education.
- The investment process is institutional: an **Investment Policy Statement (IPS)**, strategic and tactical asset allocation, manager selection and due diligence, monitoring, and an **investment committee**. UBS reports 60% of family offices operate investment committees and 68% have formal performance measurement [3].
- Costs are high and scale with assets: J.P. Morgan reports average annual operating costs of US$0.9m (≤US$250m in assets) up to US$6.6m (≥US$1bn); Latin American offices average about US$2m [5]. Total wealth management costs, including outside managers, usually exceed 100 bps of investable assets [8].
- Typical allocation (UBS 2025): US family offices hold 54% in alternatives (27% private equity, 18% real estate); Latin American offices hold 71% in traditional assets (33% equities, 31% fixed income) [2].
- In the US, a qualifying SFO is excluded from the Investment Advisers Act by the SEC family office rule [10]. In Brazil, family offices commonly use **holdings** (family holding companies), exclusive investment funds and offshore vehicles, all reshaped by Law 14.754/2023, Law 15.270/2025 and the progressive inheritance tax (ITCMD) [12][13][14][15]. Advisory and management activities for third parties fall under CVM Resolutions 19 and 21 [16][17].
- Big weaknesses even in professional offices: succession and governance. Only 35% have a defined succession plan for the office itself (UBS 2026) and 86% lack clear succession plans for decision makers (J.P. Morgan 2026) [3][5].

## 1. Definitions

| Type | What it is | Typical client | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-family office (SFO) | Dedicated company owned and controlled by one family, serving only that family | One family, often multi-generational | Maximum control and privacy, highest cost |
| Multi-family office (MFO) | Firm that serves several families with shared staff and systems | Families that cannot justify or do not want a full SFO | Usually a regulated advisory firm; fees in bps of assets |
| Virtual / outsourced family office | Small core team (or one coordinator) that hires external specialists (OCIO, accountants, lawyers) | Families wanting SFO-like coordination at lower cost | An **Outsourced Chief Investment Officer (OCIO)** is a firm that runs the investment function under contract; 22% of BlackRock's surveyed SFOs have used or would consider one [7] |
| Embedded family office | Wealth functions run inside the family's operating company | Founders still running their business | Common in Brazil and Latin America; risks mixing business and family finances |

**Wealth thresholds.** There is no legal threshold. **Estimate:** practitioners often cite investable wealth of roughly US$100m or more as the point at which a full SFO becomes cost-effective, and roughly US$20–100m as typical MFO territory. The cost data support this: an SFO with up to US$250m in assets spends about US$0.9m per year on average [5], which is at least 36 bps of assets before any external investment fees. Survey samples are much wealthier than that threshold: UBS 2026 respondents had average net worth of US$2.7bn and offices managing an average US$1.3bn [3]; J.P. Morgan 2026 respondents averaged US$1.6bn net worth, with 37% below US$250m in assets [5].

## 2. Core functions

| Function | What it covers | Typical owner |
|---|---|---|
| Investment management | Asset allocation, manager selection, direct deals, monitoring | CIO, investment team, external managers or OCIO |
| Accounting and reporting | Bookkeeping for all entities, consolidated performance and net-worth reporting, bill pay | CFO/controller, reporting platform |
| Tax | Compliance and planning in each jurisdiction, entity-level tax, cross-border issues | Tax director plus external advisors |
| Estate and succession planning | Wills, trusts, holdings, transfer timing, next-generation ownership | General counsel plus external lawyers |
| Legal | Contracts, entity management, disputes, regulatory filings | General counsel, outside counsel |
| Risk and insurance | Property, liability, key-person, cyber, physical security, concentration risk | Risk officer, insurance brokers |
| Philanthropy | Foundations, grant making, impact investing | Philanthropy director |
| Concierge / lifestyle | Real estate, aircraft, staff, travel, art | Office manager or outsourced |
| Family governance and education | Family constitution, family council, next-gen training | CEO/family office head, consultants |

Philanthropy is significant: almost 90% of North American family offices donate, with an average of US$15m, and 81% use mission statements [4]. Cambridge Associates notes that a family office often acts as "gatekeeper and coordinator" of all wealth activities, and that no family office performs 100% of services in-house [8].

## 3. The investment process

### 3.1 Investment Policy Statement (IPS)
A written document that sets the family's objectives (return target, income needs), risk tolerance, time horizon, liquidity needs, constraints (taxes, legal, values/ESG, concentrated holdings), the asset-allocation targets and ranges, benchmarks, rebalancing rules, and who decides what. In J.P. Morgan's 2026 survey only 35% have a formal IPS; 64% have investment committees, 32% boards, 27% use external advisors for periodic portfolio reviews, and 26% have mission statements or handbooks [5].

### 3.2 Strategic and tactical asset allocation
- **Strategic asset allocation (SAA):** the long-term target mix (for example 30% equities, 20% bonds, 25% private markets) that reflects the IPS. It drives most of long-run results.
- **Tactical asset allocation (TAA):** deliberate, temporary deviations within allowed ranges to respond to market conditions.
UBS 2026 found 60% of offices planning changes to their SAA within 12 months, the highest level UBS has recorded [3].

### 3.3 Manager selection and due diligence
Families invest through external managers (funds) and directly. **Due diligence** is the structured investigation before investing: investment due diligence (strategy, track record, team, process) and operational due diligence (custody, valuation, compliance, fees, conflicts). BlackRock found 72% of family offices name high fees as a significant challenge in private markets and many report expertise gaps in private-market analytics (75%) and deal sourcing (63%) [7]. UBS 2025 found 40% see greater reliance on manager selection and active management as an effective way to improve diversification [2].

### 3.4 Monitoring and reporting
Regular performance and risk reporting versus benchmarks, compliance with IPS ranges, liquidity forecasts, and capital-call schedules for private funds. Automated reporting is spreading: 69% of North American offices use automated investment reporting systems, up from 46% a year earlier [4].

### 3.5 Investment committee
A group (family members, family office executives and often independent experts) that approves the IPS, SAA, new managers and large deals. UBS 2026: 60% operate investment committees [3]. J.P. Morgan 2026: 42% of offices have committees with both family and non-family members [5].

Staffing depends on size and outsourcing. Cambridge Associates estimates that an in-house CIO, two investment analysts and a data analyst can exceed US$1m per year in compensation, benefits and overhead, before outsourced investment costs [8]. J.P. Morgan notes intense competition for talent is pushing costs up [5].

## 6. Typical asset allocation

UBS 2025 (317 SFOs, average net worth US$2.7bn) [2]:

| Asset class | United States | Latin America | Europe (ex-CH) | Switzerland |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 32% | 33% | 30% | 34% |
| Fixed income | 9% | 31% | 15% | 13% |
| Cash | 5% | n/a | 6% | n/a |
| Private equity | 27% | 17% | 27% | 16% |
| Real estate | 18% | 6% | 11% | 12% |
| Total alternatives | 54% | 29% | 49% | 44% |

Global picture: developed-market equities averaged 26% in 2024; private markets 21%; North America 53% and Western Europe 26% of regional allocation [2]. Latin American offices allocate 64% to North America and 15% to Latin America (2025) [2]; 60% and 23% in the 2026 survey [3].

Other surveys:
- **RBC/Campden 2025 (North America):** private markets are the largest asset class at 29% of the average portfolio; 88% have private-market exposure; primary 2025 objectives were improving liquidity (48%) and de-risking (33%) [4].
- **BlackRock 2025:** alternatives about 42% of portfolios, up from 39%; 32% plan to raise private credit and 30% infrastructure [7].
- **J.P. Morgan 2026:** 72% have no gold exposure and 89% no crypto; 79% have zero infrastructure; about a third hold more than 10% cash [5].
- **UBS 2026:** 24% hold crypto, usually around 1% of the portfolio [3].

Lesson: family offices with long horizons lean into illiquid private assets, while Latin American offices remain more fixed-income heavy, consistent with high local interest rates. **Assumption:** the Brazilian principal's mix will look closer to the Latin American profile unless the IPS decides otherwise.

## 7. Structures in Brazil

**Market.** One 2025 mapping of about 546 Brazilian wealth firms found 44% are MFOs, 29% SFOs and 21% wealth managers; over 65% are in São Paulo; about 10% have offices in Miami, Lisbon or Dubai; only 18% run exclusive funds [6]. ANBIMA reports R$542.3bn managed by wealth managers in 2025 [18].

**Typical building blocks:**
1. **Family holding company ("holding patrimonial")**: a company that owns the family's assets and stakes. It centralizes management, separates control from economic rights (for example voting vs. preferred shares, or donating shares while keeping usufruct, the right to use and receive income), and organizes succession. It does **not** avoid inheritance tax: donating shares is itself a taxable event [15].
2. **Exclusive investment funds ("fundos exclusivos")**: funds with a single investor (or one family). **Assumption (verify):** under CVM Resolution 175 these are classes reserved for a single professional investor. Since Law 14.754/2023, they are subject to "come-cotas" (semiannual withholding tax in May and November) at 15%, or 20% for short-term funds, ending prior tax deferral; past income was taxed at 15%, or 8% under an early-payment option [12].
3. **Offshore companies and trusts**: Law 14.754/2023 taxes profits of controlled foreign entities at 15% annually on 31 December (with an option to treat them as transparent) and treats trust assets as owned by the settlor until distribution or death, when transfer is treated as a donation or inheritance [12].
4. **Dividends**: Law 15.270/2025 introduced, from January 2026, 10% withholding on dividends above R$50,000 per month from the same company to a resident individual, 10% on dividends sent abroad, and a minimum income tax of up to 10% for annual income above R$600,000 (10% flat above R$1.2m), with a transition rule for profits through 2025 approved by 31 December 2025 [13][14].
5. **Inheritance and gift tax (ITCMD)**: Constitutional Amendment 132/2023 made state ITCMD mandatorily progressive; the maximum rate remains 8% (Senate Resolution 9/1992); Complementary Law 227/2026 requires holding shares to be valued at the real value of underlying assets, not book value, and regulates ITCMD on foreign assets, though collection depends on state laws [15].

**CVM rules for advisors and managers.**
- **Resolution 19 (securities consultants):** consulting is professional, independent, individualized guidance whose adoption and implementation are exclusively the client's. Pure financial planners (succession, pensions) and producers of management/control reports that do not recommend securities are outside the rule. Consultants must disclose conflicts, pass benefits to the client, may not receive payments that impair independence (professional investors can waive this), and automated or algorithmic consulting is fully covered, with source code available to CVM [16].
- **Resolution 21 (portfolio managers):** professional management of securities portfolios on behalf of investors requires CVM authorization as "gestor de recursos" (asset manager) and/or "administrador fiduciário" (fiduciary administrator). Legal entities need Brazilian headquarters, a statutory director responsible for management, a director for compliance and, for managers, a director for risk [17].
- **Practical consequence:** MFOs that recommend or manage securities for clients are typically registered under Resolution 19 or 21. **Assumption (verify with counsel):** an SFO managing only its own family's assets through its own holding is generally not treated as providing services to third parties; the rules found here do not state an explicit family-office exemption.

## 8. Structures in the USA

- **SEC family office rule (Rule 202(a)(11)(G)-1):** a family office is excluded from the Investment Advisers Act if it (1) has no clients other than family clients, (2) is wholly owned by family clients and exclusively controlled by family members and/or family entities, and (3) does not hold itself out to the public as an investment adviser [10]. "Family members" are lineal descendants of a common ancestor up to 10 generations, plus spouses; "family clients" also include key employees, certain trusts, estates, charities funded by family clients and family-owned companies [10]. Adopted in 2011 under the Dodd-Frank Act [11].
- **Common structures (Assumption, general practice):** a family office management company organized as an LLC (limited liability company); family investment partnerships or LLCs that pool family assets; revocable and irrevocable trusts for estate planning; private foundations or donor-advised funds for philanthropy. The rule explicitly recognizes trusts, estates and family-owned companies as family clients [10].
- **MFOs and wealth managers** serving unrelated families must register as investment advisers (SEC or state) and owe fiduciary duty. **Assumption:** standard US law; not separately cited here.

## 9. Lessons an AI version should copy

1. **Start with an IPS.** Only 35% of offices have one [5]; the AI office should require one before giving allocation advice.
2. **Institutional governance.** Mirror an investment committee: one agent proposes, another challenges, the principal approves, and all is minuted. Family offices that use AI keep final investment decisions with experienced humans [9].
3. **Consolidated reporting first.** Manual processes and spreadsheets are the top operational risk [4]; reliable reporting is the foundation.
4. **Cost discipline.** Track all-in costs in bps, because investment costs are usually more than half of total spend [8].
5. **Coordinate, don't replace, specialists.** No office does everything in-house [8]; the AI office prepares briefs for lawyers, accountants and managers.
6. **Fix the known weaknesses.** Succession, governance and next-generation education are the weakest areas (only 27% have structured heir education per UBS 2026) [3]; the AI office should keep these on the agenda.
7. **Jurisdiction-aware tax.** Brazil's rules changed sharply in 2023–2026 [12][13][15]; every structural recommendation must be checked against current law and a professional.
8. **Independence.** Avoid product conflicts, consistent with CVM rules on consultant independence [16].

## Open questions and assumptions

- **Estimate:** wealth thresholds (about US$100m for SFO, US$20–100m for MFO) are practitioner rules of thumb, not cited from a primary source.
- **Assumption (verify):** exclusive fund definition under CVM Resolution 175 and professional-investor criteria; not confirmed from the regulation text in this research.
- **Assumption (verify):** whether a Brazilian SFO managing only family assets needs CVM registration; Resolution 21 text reviewed here does not state an explicit exemption.
- **Open:** state-level ITCMD rates and implementation of Complementary Law 227/2026 in the principal's state.
- **Open:** Brazil-US tax treaty absence and US tax status of the principal (student visa vs. residency) need professional review. **Assumption:** there is no comprehensive Brazil-US income tax treaty; verify.
- **Open:** survey figures come from bank-client samples with different methodologies; treat as indicative.
