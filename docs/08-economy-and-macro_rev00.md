# Economy and Macro
> **Purpose:** Give the agent team a shared framework, indicator set, dated snapshot and reporting routine for macroeconomic analysis of Brazil, the USA and the world. **Primary users:** Chief Economist / Macro agent, Portfolio Strategist, Risk Manager, Fixed Income and FX agents, and the Sector agents for energy and hospitality.

## Summary
- Macro analysis here rests on a few lenses: the **business cycle**, the **growth/inflation regime** (a four-quadrant map), **monetary policy** (Fed and the BCB's Copom), **fiscal sustainability**, the **yield curve and credit spreads**, the **dollar cycle**, **commodity cycles** and **China's pull on Brazil**.
- **Snapshot (as of 2026-11-27):** the US is in a hot-growth, sticky-inflation phase. The Fed **raised** rates in September 2026 to 3.75%–4.00% and held in October, and markets price another hike on 9 December [1][2][3]. The 10-year Treasury yield is near 4.85%–4.95% [8].
- **Brazil:** the Selic is 13.75% after five cuts. Copom paused on 4 November 2026 [9]. 12-month IPCA inflation is 4.63%, above the 4.5% tolerance ceiling [11]. Gross debt is 82.9% of GDP [14]. The real sold off after President Lula's re-election and then recovered to about R$5.51/US$ when Luiz Carlos Trabuco was named finance minister [15][16].
- **Energy shock:** the 2026 Iran war and restrictions in the Strait of Hormuz pushed Brent to a 52-week high of about $126 in April. Brent was roughly $91–97 in late November [19][20]. Oil is the main global inflation swing factor right now.
- Macro forecasting has a poor record. Economists missed most recessions, even late in the year they happened [24]. The team should express views as **probability-weighted scenarios with signposts**, not point forecasts.

## 1. A framework for macro analysis

### 1.1 Business cycle
The business cycle is the repeating pattern of expansion, slowdown, recession and recovery in economic activity. Track it with **coincident** indicators (data that move with the economy now: payrolls, industrial production, IBC-Br), **leading** indicators (data that tend to move before it: ISM new orders, building permits, the yield curve, credit spreads, the Focus survey's revisions) and **lagging** indicators (unemployment, core inflation). In the US, the NBER dates recessions after the fact. In Brazil, CODACE/FGV does the same job.

### 1.2 Growth/inflation regimes (four-quadrant model)
Bridgewater's "All Weather" approach groups environments by whether growth and inflation are **rising or falling relative to expectations** [26]. Each quadrant tends to favor different assets.

| Regime | Growth vs expected | Inflation vs expected | Assets that tend to do well | Tends to struggle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Goldilocks | Rising | Falling | Equities, credit | Cash, commodities |
| Reflation / overheating | Rising | Rising | Commodities, value equities, EM, inflation-linked bonds | Long nominal bonds |
| Stagflation | Falling | Rising | Commodities, gold, cash, inflation-linked bonds | Equities, long bonds |
| Deflationary slowdown | Falling | Falling | Long government bonds, quality equities, USD | Commodities, credit, EM |

**Estimate (agent judgment):** in late 2026 the US is in **reflation/overheating**: growth was 3.4% annualized in Q3 and inflation is around 3.7%–3.9% and rising [4][5][6]. Brazil is closer to **stagflation-lite**: growth is slowing to about 1.7% with inflation above target [12].

### 1.3 Monetary policy
- **Fed:** dual mandate (maximum employment and 2% PCE inflation). The FOMC meets 8 times a year. Its quarterly **Summary of Economic Projections (SEP)** includes the **dot plot**, a chart of each official's expected policy rate [7][27].
- **BCB/Copom:** single mandate of a **3% continuous inflation target** with a ±1.5 pp tolerance band (ceiling 4.5%), measured by IPCA. Copom meets 8 times a year [28]. The "neutral" real rate (the rate that neither stimulates nor slows the economy) is estimated to be far higher in Brazil than in the US. That is why the Selic is structurally high.
- **Transmission:** policy rate → short yields → credit conditions, currency and asset prices → demand → inflation, with lags of roughly 6–18 months (**Assumption:** a standard textbook range).

### 1.4 Fiscal policy and debt sustainability
The core debt equation: the change in debt/GDP ≈ (r − g) × debt/GDP − primary balance, where *r* is the effective interest rate on the debt, *g* is nominal GDP growth, and the **primary balance** is the budget result before interest payments. When r > g, a government must run primary surpluses just to keep debt stable.
- **Brazil:** the **arcabouço fiscal** (fiscal framework, Complementary Law 200/2023) caps real spending growth at 70% of real revenue growth, within a 0.6%–2.5% band. It sets primary-balance targets with a ±0.25 pp of GDP tolerance band [29]. With the Selic near 14% and nominal growth near 6%–7%, r − g is strongly positive. Nominal interest added 0.8 pp to debt/GDP in September 2026 alone [14].
- **US:** the FY2026 deficit was $2.0 trillion, or 6.2% of GDP. Debt held by the public reached 100% of GDP, the highest since 1946 [21].

### 1.5 Yield curve and credit spreads
- An inverted US curve (10-year yield below the 3-month yield) has preceded every US recession since the late 1960s. The NY Fed publishes a recession probability model built on it [30].
- Credit spreads, especially the **excess bond premium** (the part of corporate spreads not explained by default risk), predict activity [31]. Watch US high-yield option-adjusted spreads (OAS) and Brazil's CDS and DI-curve slope.

### 1.6 Dollar cycle
A strong dollar tightens global financial conditions. It raises EM debt costs, lowers commodity prices in dollar terms and pressures the BRL. Drivers: US rate differentials, US relative growth and risk appetite. The DXY (an index of the dollar against major currencies) hit a 20-month high of about 104.5 in November 2026 on hawkish Fed expectations [8].

### 1.7 Commodity cycles and China's impact on Brazil
Brazil's terms of trade (export prices relative to import prices) follow soy, iron ore and oil. China took **28.7%** of Brazilian exports in 2025. About 80% of soybean exports and 45% of crude oil exports went there [23]. Chinese property weakness and deflation-driven exporting reduce demand for iron ore and add global disinflation pressure. Strong Chinese demand for food and energy supports the real. US–China trade truces shift soybean market share between the US and Brazil [23].

## 4. How macro links to asset prices
- **Equities** = earnings growth × valuation. Growth surprises lift earnings. Rising real yields compress valuations, hitting long-duration growth stocks hardest.
- **Bonds** price expected policy rates plus a term premium (extra yield investors demand for holding long bonds). Fiscal stress widens the term premium. That is the likely channel behind the US 10-year near 5% while debt is at 100% of GDP [8][21].
- **FX:** the BRL responds to the rate differential (carry), terms of trade, fiscal credibility and global risk appetite. The October 2026 sell-off and late-November rebound show that the **fiscal-credibility channel** can dominate a 10 pp carry advantage within days [15][16].
- **Commodities:** supply shocks (Hormuz) versus demand (China, global growth). In November 2026 analysts saw a possible 2027 surplus even with the war risk [19].
- **Brazil-specific:** the DI curve and NTN-B real yields are the main gauge of fiscal fear. Watch the long end (for example the January 2033 DI contract) for signs of fiscal dominance, the situation in which high debt prevents the central bank from controlling inflation.

## 5. How hard macro forecasting is
- An IMF study of 63 countries found that consensus forecasts missed the great majority of recessions, and forecasts were revised only slowly even as the recession year progressed [24].
- The Philadelphia Fed's Survey of Professional Forecasters keeps a long public record that agents can use to measure typical consensus errors [25]. **Assumption:** errors on one-year-ahead US GDP are often 1–2 pp. Agents should compute the actual figure from the SPF error statistics before quoting it.
- Live 2026 examples: the Fed went from cuts in 2025 to a **hike** in September 2026 [1]. The Focus 2026 Selic median moved from 13.25% to 13.75% within weeks [12]. Markets priced a Copom cut in November, and Copom held instead [9]. Markets also expected a Flávio Bolsonaro win, and the opposite happened [15].
- Practical lesson (Tetlock's forecasting research): break big questions into smaller ones, start from base rates (how often similar events happened historically), update in small steps and keep score with Brier scores (a measure of how close probability forecasts came to actual outcomes) [35].

## 6. How the agents should produce macro views
1. **Scenarios, not points.** Use 3–4 scenarios with probabilities that add to 100%, each with an asset-price map.
2. **Leading indicators first.** Weight ISM new orders, claims, curve slope, credit spreads, Focus revisions and oil.
3. **Signposts.** For each scenario, name 2–3 observable data points and thresholds that would raise or lower its probability.
4. **Score yourself.** Log every probability, and review Brier scores quarterly.
5. **Separate data from interpretation**, and cite the release for every number.

## 7. Sector macro drivers for the principal's businesses

### 7.1 Energy
- **Oil and fuels:** Brent moves Petrobras pricing policy, diesel and gasoline costs and Brazil's IPCA. A Brent 52-week range of about $91–126 makes hedging policy a key decision [19][20].
- **Power:** Brazil's hydro reservoirs set the **bandeira tarifária** (a monthly surcharge on electricity bills). ANEEL set a green flag for December 2026, the third green month in a row, because rains refilled reservoirs [36]. Yellow adds R$1.88/100 kWh, and red level 1 adds R$4.46/100 kWh [36].
- **Rates:** project finance for generation and transmission is highly sensitive to the Selic and NTN-B real yields. A 13.75% Selic raises hurdle rates.
- **Geopolitics:** Hormuz, the Venezuelan recovery and OPEC+ decisions (see file 09).

### 7.2 Hospitality
- **FX:** a weak BRL attracts foreign tourists and keeps Brazilians traveling at home. Brazil received 7.59 million foreign tourists from January to October 2026, the second-best such period on record, after a record 9.3 million in 2025. International tourism revenue was a record US$8.3 billion in the first nine months [37].
- **Domestic demand:** real wages, consumer credit costs (Selic), household debt service and confidence. Household consumption fell 0.4% q/q in Q2 2026 [13].
- **Costs:** energy tariffs, food inflation and labor costs. Financing for expansions is constrained by high rates.
- **US exposure:** US travel demand and a strong dollar affect any US-based operations or US-source guests.
